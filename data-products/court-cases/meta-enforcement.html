Four pivots in the enforcement story

2012
The U.S. baseline
The FTC's consent order, which requires no monetary payment, establishes the framework for the privacy program and includes a $5 billion penalty for any subsequent breach.
2019
The $5 billion reset
FTC and DOJ enforce a historic privacy settlement with governance reforms, while SEC fines $100M for delayed disclosures, totaling $5.1B in one year.
2021 – 2023
The Irish GDPR wave
Dublin emerges as Meta's primary hub for hefty fines, including WhatsApp transparency, Instagram children's data, scraping-by-design, improper legal basis for behavioral ads, and the €1.2B transfers ruling.
2024 – 2025
Global, and beyond privacy
Texas biometrics ($1.4B) and EU Marketplace fines, alongside security cases in Nigeria and India, set the stage for DMA's 'pay-or-consent' fine in 2025 and Spain's private-damages ruling, sparking new legal discussions.
04

What the cases share

THEME 01

Data maximization as risk amplifier

Germany, Türkiye, India, Korea, the Irish ad cases, and the DMA decision all oppose the aggregation of data from Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and the broader internet without a meaningful opt-out. The common critique is that the push to expand the data pool surpassed legitimate consent and fair competition under various laws.

THEME 02

Transparency & legal basis

The biggest Irish cases do not claim a hack. Instead, they argue that Meta either did not sufficiently explain their processing methods or used the incorrect legal basis for monetization - citing WhatsApp transparency, Facebook/Instagram legal decisions, and transatlantic transfers. These issues pertain to the consent and policy framework of the advertising model.

THEME 03

Security & accountability by design

The DPC deemed architectural and process weaknesses, such as scraping plaintext passwords and the access-token breach, as sanctionable even in the absence of proven external misuse. A controller of Meta's magnitude is expected to foresee potential abuse scenarios and proactively defend against them.

THEME 04

Data translated into market power

Competition authorities reframed data practices as market-structure harm, focusing on dominance, barriers to entry, self-preferencing, and tying in display advertising and classifieds, instead of simply following the lead of privacy regulators. Data is now seen as both an input and advantage, sometimes even serving as a barrier to entry.

THEME 05

"Free" services aren't free

Italy's AGCM, Nigeria's FCCPC, and Spain's publishers all agree: users are paying with their data. Spain takes it a step further, transforming what was considered illegal data exploitation into an unfair advertising advantage that harmed competitors' earnings. This resulted in a private law damages settlement based on a violation of GDPR.

THEME 06

What hasn't hit yet

As of June 2026, Meta's operations included content moderation, labor, and tax efforts within its boundaries, which had not yet resulted in significant monetary penalties. Instead, there were preliminary DSA findings, a disputed Italian VAT concept, and continuing litigation in Kenya. The primary focus of the closed-case cost center remains prioritizing privacy over competition.

Bottom line

Meta's closed-case bill is multi-billion-dollar, and overwhelmingly about data.

The portfolio is dominated by enforcement of privacy and data protection linked to failures in ad-tech, data merging, and governanceCompetition law is considered a secondary concern, with the main focus remaining on the significant monetary risks in the multi-billion dollar range. This conclusion holds steady despite potential currency fluctuations, particularly due to the 2019 U.S. reset and the upcoming 2024 global wave.

The newest matters point forward. The DMA "pay-or-consent" fine and Spain's private-damages judgment Enforcement is shifting from state fines to gatekeeper-style structural duties and civil liability towards competitors, targeting the ad-supported model more effectively than any individual penalty.

This record focuses on major cases and is not a comprehensive overview. Smaller cases, such as Ireland's €5.5M 2023 WhatsApp decision and Korea's 2024 sensitive-data fine, could slightly increase the overall numbers. Numerous significant cases are still undergoing appeals, including decisions from the EU Marketplace and DMA, the Spanish judgment, India's WhatsApp case, and various Irish GDPR fines.
