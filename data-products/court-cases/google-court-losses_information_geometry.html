Google's most consequential losses cluster in competition law: Google has faced significant legal setbacks in the EU, US, India, and Australia, with courts consistently ruling in favor of the theory that Google used its control of a crucial access point to give preference to its own services, shut out competitors, or maintain a monopoly. What the cases share

PATTERN 01

Control of the gateway

In Shopping, it was the results page; in Android and US Search, the path that preloads apps and sets defaults; in Epic and India, the app store and billing bottleneck; in ad tech, the combined control of ad-serving and the exchange. Google faced legal challenges for leveraging its existing bottleneck to stifle competition.

PATTERN 02

Fines give way to remedies

The case between Google Spain and CNIL shifted obligations regarding delisting and consent. Recent competition cases have pushed boundaries even more: the DC search ruling alters Google's contracts and requires competitor access; the Epic injunction promotes Play Store transparency; India focuses on billing and distribution. The potential risk ahead is not just financial, but also entails redesigning business models.

PATTERN 03

Doctrines converge

The terminology may vary - Article 102 in the EU, Section 2 in the US, Section 4 in India, consumer law in Australia - but the issues remain consistent: self-preferencing, tying, discriminatory access, default-placement payments, foreclosure by design. Gatekeeper theories from the EU are gaining traction globally, with US courts now adopting remedies previously seen as European.

Convergence is geometric

The defaults-and-preload cases from the EU, India, the US, and Australia are all closely connected as mutual nearest neighbors. Jurisdiction is de-emphasized and borders fade away, leaving behind a unified bundling ridge that each of the four legal systems has entered independently.

FINDING 03

A slope from pay to rebuild

Changing the color to remedy brings a gradient to the competition basin: fines on one side and structural conduct orders on the other. The manifold tips, with the most recent rulings all leaning towards the "re-architect" side.

FINDING 04

Two basins, one defendant

The area is not consistent. Legal decisions gravitate towards two main influences - a competitive area and a private/intermediary area - with consent cases closely intertwined. Google's legal exposure is spread across separate territories, rather than concentrated in one place.

Bottom line

Google's legal exposure now spans across multiple areas of law.

Now, its significant losses have become a unified body of legal principles throughout competition, privacy and consumer protection, and intermediary obligations however, the focal point remains on antitrust concerns. Courts are now viewing Google's ecosystem design decisions as exclusionary, specifically when they force competitors to rely on gateways controlled by Google. This trend is consistent throughout. Shopping and Android to DOJ Search, Epic, and the Indian cases.

Two risk trends follow. Distribution deals and default placements are increasingly becoming a major risk in legal disputes, particularly when combined with payments or conditioning across multiple products. remedies are hardening focused on unlocking interfaces, separating defaults, and empowering competition. This poses a more significant long-term danger than hefty fines alone.
