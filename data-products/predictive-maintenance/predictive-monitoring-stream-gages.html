Predictive Monitoring for Stream Gages

By Prashant Dhingra | Use Cases & Industry

Using AI/ML to Estimate Waterflow When Sensors Fail

Saving Lives and Reducing Infrastructure Costs

The Emergency Response Challenge

Accurate, real-time waterflow data is crucial for emergency response teams to assess flood risk during storms and floods, but the current infrastructure for collecting this vital information is hindered by operational and financial obstacles.

The Current Infrastructure Problem

💰 Massive Infrastructure Investment

The US government has allocated $20 billion for the installation of stream gages nationwide. Each gage has an estimated cost of $50,000, and there are annual operational expenses of $200 million to sustain the network.

⚠️ Frequent Sensor Failures

Gauges frequently malfunction, especially during floods and storms when they are most necessary. The sensors are often damaged or destroyed by floating trees and debris in the river.

🚨 Data When It Matters Most

During flood and storm emergencies, emergency response teams require immediate access to waterflow data to identify at-risk areas. Unfortunately, gages are prone to failure or damage during these critical moments.

❌ Why Traditional Approaches Fail

Gages are not suitable for predictive maintenance as they can fail unexpectedly in harsh conditions. The complex relationships between weather, snow, temperature, and dam operations make forecasting waterflow directly very challenging.

The Core Insight

The issue lies not in predicting the failure of gauges or forecasting waterflow in isolation. Rather, it involves staying aware of waterflow levels even in the event of sensor malfunctions. The solution is to employ machine learning to approximate waterflow at non-functional gauges using data from nearby operational gauges.

The ML Solution: Waterflow Estimation

We utilize machine learning to rely on connections between neighboring gages instead of forecasting gage failures or exact waterflow. By analyzing data from related gages in the vicinity, we can accurately estimate waterflow when a gage malfunctions.

The ML Approach

Traditional Stream Flow Monitoring

  • Stream gage collects waterflow data every 15 minutes
  • Data made available to government agencies in real-time
  • But gages break frequently, especially during floods and storms
  • Critical data becomes unavailable at the worst possible time

ML-Powered Waterflow Estimation

  • Create clusters of gages with similar waterflow patterns
  • Identify correlations between gages within clusters
  • If a gauge malfunctions, utilize cluster correlations to approximate its waterflow
  • Achieve >90% accuracy::emergency teams get reliable data when needed most

Key Advantages

✓ Unprecedented Accuracy

Government agencies can still acquire highly accurate waterflow information, with a success rate of over 90%, even in cases where gages malfunction, ensuring the dependable data essential for making emergency response decisions.

✓ Significant Cost Savings

Organizations can significantly reduce costs on emergency repairs and operational expenses by ensuring system visibility without depending solely on flawless sensor availability.

✓ Safer Emergency Response

Emergency responders can use their knowledge to assess flood risk even in the absence of functioning sensors. When conditions are hazardous, there is no need for teams to endanger lives by replacing faulty gauges.

✓ Practical Deployment

The solution doesn't necessitate the replacement or upkeep of all gages. It operates effectively with existing gages by making intelligent estimations to prevent failures.

Real-World Impact

In flood-prone regions like Alaska, where gages often fail during winter and storm seasons, teams can now avoid risking lives by using a reliable ML system to estimate waterflow. This enables emergency response decisions to be made even when gages are not functioning.

Understanding Data Patterns for ML Success

Creating a successful ML model hinges on recognizing the patterns present in your data. Straightforward patterns call for basic models, while intricate patterns call for more advanced techniques.

Three Pattern Complexity Levels

1
Obvious Patterns

Data Characteristics: Clear, distinct patterns are visible

Requirements: Need less data, fewer features, simple models

Expected Outcome: Highly accurate results with straightforward approaches

Example: Strong seasonal waterflow patterns with clear peaks and valleys

2
Less Obvious Patterns

Data Characteristics: Patterns exist but are subtle or multi-layered

Requirements: Need more historical data, additional features, complex models

Expected Outcome: Good accuracy with sophisticated modeling approaches

Example: Waterflow influenced by multiple correlated factors (weather, temperature, reservoir levels)

3
Even Less Obvious Patterns

Data Characteristics: Patterns are highly complex or non-linear

Requirements: Different algorithms, many features, complex or deep learning models

Expected Outcome: Moderate accuracy::inherent complexity limits predictability

Example: Extreme events, unusual weather combinations with unprecedented impacts

Key Data Exploration Insight

The complexity of your patterns determines your approach. Begin by exploring your data before assuming that deep learning or intricate models are necessary. Simple, interpretable models can often uncover obvious patterns, while more complex ones may be needed for intricate patterns, but may have their limits in predictability. Make sure to understand your data thoroughly before selecting a model.

ML Model Architecture for Waterflow Prediction

The ML model structure includes four key stages: collecting data and performing correlation analysis, training the model, managing flooding events, and making real-time predictions. This methodical process guarantees precision and dependability in critical situations.

Four-Phase Model Architecture

1
Phase 1: Correlation Analysis & Clustering

Compute once per year: Examine stream data from NWIS to cluster gages with similar time series patterns, pinpointing gages with matching surge or decline trends in waterflow. Identify regions lacking clustering (possible coverage gaps).

Output: Gage clusters with strong correlations ready for modeling

2
Phase 2: ML Training with SHEM

Compute once per year: Develop machine learning models for each gage utilizing SHEM (Streamgage Hydrologic Estimate Using Machine Learning) to differentiate between easily predictable gages with strong correlations and more challenging gages with weak correlations in remote areas. Create reliability models based on past data and validate them across various datasets.

Output: Trained models with confidence scores for each gage

3
Phase 3: Dynamic Model Application

Real-time execution: If a stream gage malfunctions, locate operational gages within the same group. If necessary, update the model in real-time with new data to ensure forecasts adjust to present circumstances instead of just past trends.

Output: Adaptive models responsive to current conditions

4
Phase 4: Real-Time Prediction & Insights

On-demand execution: Forecast waterflow for malfunctioning gauges by utilizing operational gauges within the group. Obtain key findings on necessary enhancements or new components to enhance precision and coverage. Enhance the system continuously by evaluating prediction effectiveness.

Output: Real-time waterflow estimates for emergency response teams

Model Architecture Advantages
  • Correlation-Based: Utilizes geographical and hydrological relationships instead of attempting to forecast exact water flow volumes.
  • Clustering: Groups similar gages for more accurate predictions within homogeneous regions
  • Adaptive: Retrains dynamically when needed to account for changing conditions
  • Scalable: One model per gage, enabling parallel training and prediction
  • Transparent: Identifies easy vs. difficult to predict gages, guiding improvement efforts

Model Results & Performance

The ML model shows different accuracy levels for different gages, with identifiable patterns that help explain why some locations are more predictable than others. Recognizing these patterns is key to enhancing the model further.

Three Categories of Results

Category 1: Accurate Predictions

Performance: Excellent correlation between predicted and actual waterflow

Explanation: Strong geographic/hydrologic correlations with nearby gages enable accurate estimation

Characteristics: Gages with multiple similar neighbors in the cluster

Use Case: Safe to use for emergency response decisions

Category 2: Good with Limitations

Performance: Accurate predictions for gradual changes, struggles with sharp declines

Explanation: Models capture general trends but miss rapid state changes

Characteristics: Normal conditions well-modeled, extreme events less accurate

Use Case: Use with caution during extreme weather; supplement with additional features

Category 3: Needs Improvement

Performance: Moderate accuracy overall

Explanation: Insufficient training data, low correlations with nearby gages, or uncommon local conditions

Improvement Path: Candidate for deep learning approaches or additional feature engineering

Action Items: Gather additional historical data and uncover more predictive features such as weather, elevation, and vegetation.

Key Insights from Results

What the Results Tell Us
  • Clustering Works: Gages with strong geographic/hydrologic correlations yield accurate predictions
  • Pattern Complexity Varies: Certain locations display clear patterns that can be effectively modeled using simple methods, while others exhibit more intricate patterns that require sophisticated approaches.
  • Extreme Events Challenge: Models perform adequately under usual conditions but encounter challenges when faced with sudden, significant changes.
  • Geographic Factors Matter: Isolated gages or unique hydrologic conditions reduce predictability
  • Feature Engineering Opportunity: Incorporating external data such as weather, temperature, and reservoir levels may enhance forecasts for challenging gauges.
Practical Impact on Emergency Response

Although the system may not be completely accurate on all gages, it still offers decision support for emergency teams. The system's high accuracy rate on predictable gages is beneficial for numerous critical locations. In instances where gages are difficult to predict, the system alerts teams to potential risks and uncertainties, allowing them to take necessary precautions. When paired with manual inspection, the machine learning estimates greatly enhance situational awareness during floods and storms.

Implementation Framework for Predictive Monitoring

Developing a predictive monitoring system involves a methodical process that integrates technical advancements, operational integration, and ongoing enhancements.

Implementation Phases

1
Data Exploration & Pattern Analysis

Gain insights from your data by examining historical waterflow patterns at each gage, identifying clusters of gages with similar patterns, and evaluating pattern complexity to determine the suitability of simple or complex models.

2
Clustering & Correlation Analysis

Apply time series similarity metrics for gage cluster identification, calculate correlations among gages in each cluster, and pinpoint areas of weak or absent clustering. Document the predictability of individual gages as either easy or challenging.

3
Model Selection & Training

Begin with basic regression or time series models for simple gages that are easy to predict. Utilize more advanced models only when necessary due to the complexity of the data. Update models yearly using the complete historical dataset and test them on separate data to assess their real-world effectiveness.

4
Operational Integration

Utilize emergency response systems to incorporate real-time waterflow estimates in the event of gage failure. Show confidence scores to assist teams in gauging reliability and establish alerts for when critical gages are not accessible. Conduct training for emergency response teams on utilizing these estimates.

5
Continuous Improvement

Track the accuracy of predictions in real-world scenarios, pinpointing cases where errors occur systematically. Gather additional data to potentially enhance accuracy, continuously improving models. Extend analysis to challenging locations by collecting targeted data and refining features.

Success Factors

Keys to Implementation Success

Start with Data Understanding: Don't assume you need complex models. Explore your data first.

Leverage Correlations: Geographic and hydrologic correlations are more predictable than absolute values.

Identify Easy vs Hard: Segment gages by predictability. Use simpler approaches where they work.

Build Iteratively: Begin by addressing 80% of locations, enhance to 90%, and then tackle the more challenging remaining cases.

Integrate Operationally: The top model is only beneficial when utilized by emergency responders.

Making Predictive Monitoring Work for Your Organization

Predictive monitoring demonstrates how ML solves real operational problems. The real challenge lies in staying alert to your surroundings even when sensors are not perfect. By using correlations between neighboring sensors, you can make accurate estimates of missing data.

The key to success is pragmatism. Begin by exploring the data to identify patterns, whether they are clear or subtle. Create basic models when possible, opting for more complex methods only when necessary. Collaborate with operations to ensure that predictions are effectively implemented.

The value is substantial and multifaceted. Cut expenses by preventing last-minute repairs. Enhance safety by avoiding risky sensor swaps. Boost emergency readiness with dependable data in crucial moments. And most importantly: protect lives by providing decision-makers with critical information.

Predictive monitoring isn't theoretical::it's practical, deployable, and impactful today. No matter if you are overseeing water systems, power grids, infrastructure networks, or any distributed monitoring system, these principles remain relevant. The key is to utilize machine learning to predict missing sensor data based on related data points, stay informed even during failures, and provide value when necessary.
