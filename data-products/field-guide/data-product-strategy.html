Strategy · Leadership and investment

Build a data product strategy that connects to business decisions

A data product strategy sets priorities for the decisions and workflows the organization wants to improve. It also establishes who owns each offering, how shared capabilities are funded, and what evidence will guide continued investment.

By Prashant Dhingra·4 min read·November 2026 edition

Keep these ideas in mind

  • Prioritize a recurring business problem with a named consumer.
  • Give owners authority and capacity for the full product lifecycle.
  • Review value, adoption, reliability, and cost together.

Start with a decision the organization wants to improve

A useful strategy begins with a small number of business outcomes and the decisions that influence them. Ask where people repeatedly wait for information, reconcile conflicting definitions, or act without adequate evidence. Describe the current workflow, its constraints, and the consequence of getting the decision wrong.

Illustrative example: a service organization wants to reduce missed customer onboarding steps. The relevant product might make account milestones, unresolved issues, and responsible teams visible before a weekly review. The strategy should explain how that information changes prioritization and follow-up, rather than assuming a new dashboard will improve customer experience by itself.

Record a baseline before development. It might be the time required to prepare the review, the proportion of accounts missing a documented next action, or the effort spent resolving inconsistent status definitions. A baseline makes the problem testable and gives the team a way to judge whether its first release helped.

Keep the intended outcome distinct from what the product can directly control. Better information can support better action; it cannot guarantee the business result.

Choose a portfolio the organization can sustain

Evaluate candidate products using value and feasibility together. A highly visible use case with unavailable data or unclear ownership may be a poor first investment. A narrower product can be a better pilot when it addresses repeated demand and exposes reusable practices without depending on a large organizational redesign.

CriterionEvidence to request
Consumer needA named team, recurring workflow, and observed friction.
Decision valueA plausible path from information to action and a measurable baseline.
Data readinessKnown sources, access rights, definitions, and material quality limitations.
Delivery feasibilityManageable dependencies and a useful first release.
Operating capacityAn accountable owner, support plan, and realistic running costs.
Reuse potentialSpecific additional consumers with compatible needs.

Use scoring to structure discussion, not to disguise uncertainty. Make assumptions visible and revisit them after discovery. Speculative reuse should not outweigh a demonstrated need. The portfolio should include maintenance and retirement work alongside new features, because every additional product creates continuing obligations.

Assign decisions across business, data, and technology leaders

Leadership responsibilities need clear boundaries. A business or product leader should represent consumer needs, own adoption, and judge whether the workflow improves. A data leader should coordinate definitions, portfolio priorities, governance, and ownership. A technology leader should ensure the platform, access mechanisms, deployment practices, reliability, and cost controls can support the commitments.

The exact titles vary. In a small organization, one person may hold several responsibilities. What matters is that the team knows who can approve a change in business meaning, who can accept a reliability tradeoff, who funds recurring operation, and who resolves a disagreement between consumers.

Give each product an accountable owner while making specialist responsibilities explicit. Ownership does not mean one person performs every engineering, security, and support task. It means someone can coordinate the work and make prioritization decisions within agreed constraints.

Shared standards should make collaboration easier. Agree on a small set of requirements for documentation, identifiers, access, quality evidence, and change communication. Use platform capabilities to automate repeatable checks where practical, while preserving human judgment about business meaning and acceptable use.

Fund an operating service, including its shared foundations

A one-time delivery budget can create an unsupported dependency for other teams. Plan for development, hosting, monitoring, incident response, documentation, consumer onboarding, and eventual migration or retirement. Make the support commitment proportional to the product's importance and the team's capacity.

Estimate costs with enough detail to guide decisions. Separate product-specific work from shared platform expenditure, and state how shared costs are allocated. A simple view of storage, compute, engineering effort, and support can be more useful initially than a complicated internal pricing model.

Choose an organizational model that fits present capability. Centralized ownership may suit a small portfolio with shared expertise. Domain ownership can improve accountability for business meaning when teams have the skills and capacity to operate their products. A hybrid model can combine domain decisions with a common platform and standards.

Expand autonomy deliberately. Moving responsibility to a domain without providing tools, training, or support transfers workload without solving the underlying problem. Treat the platform itself as a service whose consumers are the product teams, and improve it in response to repeated delivery obstacles.

Review evidence and make explicit investment decisions

Review four dimensions together: value, adoption, trust, and efficiency. Value concerns the workflow or outcome being improved. Adoption shows whether intended users rely on the product. Trust covers measured quality, freshness, and incident handling. Efficiency examines the effort and cost of providing the service.

A growing catalog is evidence of publication, not sufficient evidence of success. Equally, low usage may reflect poor discovery or onboarding rather than an unnecessary product. Investigate before deciding. Record which benefits are measured, which are estimated, and which depend on changes outside the data team's control.

  • Can intended consumers explain how the product helps their work?
  • Are reliability commitments met, with important limitations visible?
  • Does the roadmap respond to actual requests and observed friction?
  • Are ongoing costs and ownership sustainable?
  • Should the next decision be to invest, improve, maintain, combine, or retire?

Make these reviews part of portfolio management. They give leaders a reasoned way to stop low-value work, protect useful services, and direct the next investment toward evidence rather than novelty.

Common questions

Who should own the overall strategy?

Assign a leader who can connect business priorities, data responsibilities, and technology investment. The strategy requires joint decisions even when one executive is accountable for coordinating it.

How many pilot products should we start with?

Choose a number the organization can support through real consumer use. One well-scoped pilot can expose essential lessons. Add another only when it tests a meaningful difference without overwhelming operating capacity.

What should the first success metric be?

Use a measure tied to the chosen workflow, supported by adoption and reliability evidence. For example, review preparation time is more informative for an onboarding operations product than the total number of tables published.
