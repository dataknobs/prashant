01 / The festival

A harvest of light

Makar Sankranti brings together sunlight, seasonal change, gratitude, and the joy of sharing. Across India, the middle of January is filled with distinctive celebrations rooted in the land and the communities that care for it.

This visual journey follows ten slides through the stories and customs of the season—from sacred narratives to festive kitchens and kite-filled skies.

02 / Shared season

One turning point, many stories

A single season can hold many traditions. Makar Sankranti, Pongal, Uttarayan, Lohri, and Magh Bihu share themes of renewal and abundance, while each retains its own history, calendar, rituals, and regional identity.

Explore them as related celebrations rather than interchangeable names: a pot of new rice, a gathering around a bonfire, and a gift of sesame sweets each express gratitude in a different way.

03 / Light & renewal

The Sun’s journey northward

Makar Sankranti marks the Sun’s entry into Makara, or Capricorn, in the traditional sidereal zodiac. It is widely associated with Uttarayan and the promise of increasing light, making the Sun a powerful symbol of hope and fresh beginnings.

A calendar distinction: the astronomical northward turn begins at the December solstice. The January festival and the solstice are distinct today, even though their symbolism remains closely linked in tradition.

04 / An epic story

Bhishma’s vow

In the Mahabharata tradition, Bhishma has the power to choose the time of his death. Wounded on the battlefield and resting on a bed of arrows, he waits for the auspicious period of Uttarayan before departing.

Retold during the season, this sacred narrative invites reflection on resolve, duty, patience, and the meaning of an auspicious moment. It belongs to the festival’s religious and literary associations.

05 / Tamil Nadu

Pongal: the joy of boiling over

Pongal celebrates the harvest through an offering of newly harvested rice, traditionally cooked with milk and often jaggery. As the pot bubbles over, its overflowing abundance becomes a joyful wish for prosperity.

The wider celebration also honours the work that sustains a household and a farming community. Mattu Pongal gives special recognition to cattle, reflecting their place in agricultural life.

06 / Gujarat’s Uttarayan

Kites across the winter sky

In Gujarat, Uttarayan is especially associated with kite flying. Terraces become gathering places, and colourful kites transform the open sky into a shared celebration of skill, friendship, and the season.

The pleasure is as much in the company as in the flight: families share food, watch the changing sky, and cheer each other on. Choose bird-safe string and open spaces away from power lines.

07 / Punjab

Lohri: warmth around the bonfire

Lohri is celebrated around the same mid-January season, commonly on the evening before Makar Sankranti. In Punjab and neighbouring communities, its bonfires bring people together for songs, conversation, and festive food.

Sesame, peanuts, popcorn, and jaggery-based treats are familiar parts of the celebration. The fire becomes a centre of warmth and companionship during winter; Lohri has its own distinct cultural identity.

08 / Maharashtra

Til-gud: sweet words for a new season

Sesame and jaggery sweets are an especially beloved part of Makar Sankranti in Maharashtra. Exchanging til-gud turns a small gift into an invitation to kindness, reconciliation, and warmer relationships.

The familiar greeting “Til-gul ghya, god god bola” is often understood as “Accept these sesame-jaggery sweets, and speak sweetly.” The enduring message is simple: let sweetness shape both the food and the conversation.

09 / Assam

Magh Bihu: a community feast

In Assam, Magh Bihu—also called Bhogali Bihu—celebrates the harvest with food, fellowship, and traditions of gathering around fire. The season highlights the satisfaction of sharing what the fields have provided.

Uruka, the festive evening before Bihu, is associated with community feasting. Traditional meji fires and foods such as pitha and laru help give the celebration its distinctive Assamese character.

10 / A shared spirit

One Sun, one season

These celebrations speak in different languages and take different forms, yet their recurring themes are deeply familiar: gratitude for food, respect for nature, affection for family, and generosity towards neighbours.

Carry the spirit of Sankranti into everyday life: share a meal, thank someone whose work nourishes you, reconnect with a friend, and make room for a brighter beginning.

A little more to know

When is Makar Sankranti celebrated?

It usually falls on January 14 or 15. The exact observance depends on the year, local calendar, and the timing of the Sun’s entry into Makara. Check a local panchang for year-specific ritual timings.

Are Pongal, Lohri, and Magh Bihu the same festival?

They are distinct celebrations occurring in the same broad harvest season. Their shared themes include gratitude, community, and renewal, but their customs and cultural histories differ.

Does Makar Sankranti mark the winter solstice?

No. The winter solstice occurs in December. Makar Sankranti is a January observance based on the traditional sidereal solar calendar; its association with Uttarayan is a cultural and calendrical tradition.
