Why Is Dussehra Celebrated After Navratri?

Dussehra comes after Navratri because it is the tenth day that completes the nine nights of worship of the Goddess.

By Prashant Dhingra

Read the guide Jump to FAQs

In short

Dussehra comes after Navratri because it is the tenth day that completes the nine nights of worship of the Goddess. In Shakta tradition the nine nights are Durga's battle with Mahishasura and the tenth is her victory. In many Ramayana retellings Rama worships the Goddess before the final battle and defeats Ravana on the tenth day.

Cultural note: Names, stories, rituals and sequencing can differ by region, language, sampradaya, family tradition and local calendar. Where several interpretations exist, this guide presents them as traditions rather than claiming that one version is universally authoritative.

What Dussehra and Vijayadashami represent

Dussehra, Dasara and Vijayadashami are names used for closely related festival observances that fall at the culmination of the Navratri period. In many north Indian traditions, the day is strongly associated with the Ramayana narrative and Rama's victory over Ravana. In many Shakta traditions, especially in eastern India, Vijayadashami marks the conclusion of Durga Puja and is associated with Durga's victory over Mahishasura. In parts of southern and western India, the day also carries strong associations with learning, tools, vehicles, Shami worship, royal processions and auspicious beginnings.

That diversity is important here too. A custom that is central in one region may be absent in another.

Core themes

Several themes recur across Dussehra traditions:

  • the triumph of dharma over adharma;
  • courage, discipline and responsibility;
  • gratitude for knowledge, tools and livelihood;
  • respect for teachers, elders and family;
  • renewal after the nine nights of Navratri;
  • community storytelling through Ramlila, processions, music and fairs;
  • beginning a new educational, professional or personal undertaking.

The familiar phrase "victory of good over evil" is a useful summary, but the festival's stories are more layered than a simple moral slogan. Ramayana and Devi traditions contain questions of duty, power, loyalty, devotion, justice and consequence.

Dussehra, Dasara and Vijayadashami

The words are often used interchangeably in casual conversation, but regional usage differs. "Dussehra" is common in much of north and central India, "Dasara" is widely used in parts of the south and west, and "Vijayadashami" is especially useful when discussing the tenth lunar day and the culmination of Navratri or Durga Puja.

Rather than asking which term is "correct," it is more accurate to ask which term a particular community uses and what traditions it emphasizes.

How families may observe the day

A home observance can be simple: clean the puja space, light a diya, offer flowers or fruit, recite a prayer or read a short passage, seek blessings from elders, and share a festive meal. Some families worship books, instruments, tools or vehicles; others exchange Shami or Apta leaves; others attend Ramlila or Ravana Dahan; and many Bengali families participate in Bijoya Dashami visits and greetings.

There is no requirement for every household to perform every custom.

A clear way to explain the topic

Start with the modern custom, then explain the story or regional tradition behind it. Finally, identify where interpretations differ. This sequence is especially useful for students and international readers because it separates observable practice from mythology, symbolism and later interpretation.

Practical checklist

Before you finalize your plans:

  • confirm the tradition and regional context;
  • verify the festival date or local event schedule;
  • check family, temple, school or venue expectations;
  • prepare clothing, food, puja items or tickets in advance;
  • plan transportation for crowded public events;
  • keep children away from fire, fireworks and dense barriers;
  • choose reusable or low-waste decorations when possible;
  • respect photography restrictions and religious spaces;
  • keep the celebration manageable rather than over-scheduled.

Common mistakes to avoid

A frequent mistake is presenting one regional custom as if it represents all of India. Another is copying ritual instructions without knowing whether they belong to the family's own tradition.

Accuracy improves when you distinguish scripture, later retellings, folklore, local custom and modern festival practice. For public events, never trade safety for a better view or photograph.

Frequently asked questions

Why Is Dussehra Celebrated After Navratri?

Dussehra comes after Navratri because it is the tenth day that completes the nine nights of worship of the Goddess. In Shakta tradition the nine nights are Durga's battle with Mahishasura and the tenth is her victory. In many Ramayana retellings Rama worships the Goddess before the final battle and defeats Ravana on the tenth day.

Is Dussehra celebrated the same way everywhere?

No. Dussehra and Vijayadashami traditions vary substantially by region, language, family and religious tradition.

Is Dussehra the same as Vijayadashami?

The terms often refer to the same festival day, but regional usage and the traditions emphasized can differ.

Does every Dussehra celebration include Ravana Dahan?

No. Ravana Dahan is prominent in many north Indian celebrations, but many communities observe Vijayadashami without burning an effigy.

How can families keep the celebration simple?

Choose the customs that are meaningful to your household: a short puja, blessings from elders, a festive meal, reading or storytelling, or attending one community event.

What is the best way to learn the local tradition?

Ask family elders or community organizers and consult reputable temple, cultural or regional sources rather than assuming a generic checklist applies everywhere.

Final thoughts

Like every part of Dussehra and Vijayadashami, this is best understood in the festival’s wider context rather than as an isolated custom. The festival's strength lies in its diversity: families and communities can honor inherited traditions while still adapting celebration details to modern life, safety needs and local circumstances.
