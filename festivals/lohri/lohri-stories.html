01 · A festival woven from everyday life

One bonfire, many stories

Lohri brings together the warmth of a winter fire, the sweetness of seasonal food, the memory of a folk hero, and the joy of welcoming new members into a family. Its meaning lives in these overlapping traditions rather than in a single origin story.

Celebrated especially in Punjab and among Punjabi communities around the world, Lohri is commonly observed on 13 January, on the eve of Maghi. Families may give the occasion different religious or cultural meanings, but gathering, sharing, and singing remain at its heart.

02 · Courage remembered in song

The legend of Dulla Bhatti

Dulla Bhatti, also known as Rai Abdullah Bhatti, is remembered as a sixteenth-century Punjabi rebel from the time of Emperor Akbar. In Lohri folklore, he becomes a protector of vulnerable people and a champion of generosity.

Popular retellings describe him helping young women escape exploitation and arranging their marriages with dignity. Sundri and Mundri feature in familiar versions of the tale. Details vary across oral traditions; the enduring message is that courage matters most when it protects someone else. His name returns each Lohri through the song “Sundar Mundriye,” connecting the celebration to acts of kindness.

03 · A circle of warmth and gratitude

Lohri ki aag: the bonfire

At dusk, the bonfire becomes a gathering place. Families and neighbours stand around its glow, exchange greetings, sing, and share food. In many homes, sesame, rewri, peanuts, and popcorn are offered to the flames as expressions of gratitude and hope.

Some participants walk around the fire in a practice often called parikrama. Others experience the evening primarily as a community celebration. Customs differ from family to family: the shared fire can hold prayer, remembrance, conversation, and laughter all at once.

04 · Gratitude for what grows

The land, the season, and the harvest

Lohri belongs to Punjab’s agricultural calendar. Sugarcane and jaggery give the season much of its sweetness, while the standing winter crops make the festival a moment to hope for abundance in the months ahead.

Lohri is often called a harvest festival, but January is not the main wheat-harvest season in Punjab. Wheat generally matures and is harvested in spring, with Baisakhi closely associated with that later harvest. Lohri’s agricultural meaning includes both thanks for seasonal produce and hopes for crops still growing.

05 · New beginnings, warmly welcomed

A family’s first Lohri

The first Lohri after a wedding or the arrival of a baby is often celebrated with particular affection. Relatives and friends gather to offer blessings, bring gifts, and welcome the newly married couple or child into the wider circle of family life.

There is no single required way to celebrate. A joyful first Lohri can be a large gathering or a simple evening with loved ones. Many families celebrate daughters and sons with equal enthusiasm, making the occasion a welcome for every child and every new beginning.

06 · Tradition carried by young voices

Door-to-door songs and shared treats

In a familiar Lohri custom, groups of children visit neighbours and sing seasonal songs. Households respond with treats or small gifts. These exchanges make the neighbourhood part of the celebration and help children learn songs through participation.

“Sundar Mundriye” is among the best-known Lohri songs, invoking Dulla Bhatti in a lively call-and-response rhythm. Words and pronunciation can vary between families and places. The tradition survives through the pleasure of singing together, hearing an elder explain a verse, and passing it on.

07 · Hope in the heart of winter

Fire, the sun, and longer days

The bonfire invites reflection on light, renewal, and the sun’s life-giving warmth. Some Hindu interpretations connect it with Agni and Surya; other families emphasise its seasonal and communal significance. These meanings can coexist without every household following the same beliefs.

Lohri takes place after the December winter solstice, when daylight in the Northern Hemisphere has already begun to lengthen. It is not the astronomical shortest day of the year. Its closeness to Maghi and Makar Sankranti gives it a place in a wider season of mid-January celebrations.

Calendar perspective: The slide’s reference to the longest nights is seasonal symbolism. The actual winter solstice falls in December.
08 · A festival with more than one explanation

What’s in the name “Lohri”?

Several popular explanations connect the name Lohri with familiar objects, ingredients, or stories. One links it to loh, an iron griddle; another to til and rorhi, sesame and jaggery. Other accounts connect the festival with the figure of Loi.

These are traditional explanations rather than a single settled etymology. Their variety reveals how communities interpret a festival through the things they know: cooking, food, stories, and the language spoken at home.

09 · Keep the warmth moving

One fire, one spirit

The stories of Lohri return to a few simple values: share what you have, honour the land, welcome new life, and remember those who stand up for others. The sweets may disappear by the end of the evening, but the feeling of belonging can last much longer.

Whether celebrated in a Punjabi village, a city courtyard, or a gathering far from home, Lohri offers a reason to reconnect. Tell a child the story behind a song, invite someone into the circle, and make room for another voice. That is how a living tradition continues.

Sweetness to share

The flavours of Lohri

Til, gur & gajak

Sesame and jaggery bring a nutty, caramel sweetness to winter treats. Rewri and gajak are familiar favourites to pass around the gathering.

Peanuts & popcorn

Simple, generous handfuls make easy sharing food. These snacks also appear in the customary offerings made around many Lohri bonfires.

A comforting meal

Sarson da saag and makki di roti are beloved Punjabi winter foods. Family menus vary, but a shared meal gives the evening another place to gather.

A little more to know

Questions about Lohri

What is Lohri?

Lohri is a Punjabi winter folk festival celebrated with bonfires, songs, seasonal foods, and gatherings that express gratitude, community, and hope.

When is Lohri celebrated?

Lohri is commonly celebrated on 13 January, on the eve of Maghi. Consult the calendar followed by your community for a particular year.

Why is Dulla Bhatti remembered at Lohri?

Punjabi folklore remembers Dulla Bhatti as a courageous protector of vulnerable people. His story is preserved in Lohri songs, especially Sundar Mundriye.

Which foods are associated with Lohri?

Popular treats include rewri, gajak, sesame sweets, jaggery, peanuts, and popcorn. Festive meals may include sarson da saag and makki di roti.

Is Lohri the wheat harvest or winter solstice?

Lohri is connected with the agricultural year, but Punjab’s main wheat harvest comes in spring. The astronomical winter solstice occurs in December, before Lohri.
