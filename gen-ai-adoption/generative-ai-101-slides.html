Introduction

Generative AI has moved from writing text on request to doing work: researching, calling tools, running multi-step tasks, and handing results to people for review. This page is the DataKnobs Generative AI 101 deck with a written guide to go with it. It covers how the technology works, how to build with it, which models and vendors matter right now, and how to adopt it without losing control of quality, cost or risk.

Generative AI 101 deck title: overview, history, current state, what's coming, limitations and mitigationsGenerative AI 101 agenda: technology, applications, vendors, risks and adoptionGenerative AI drives creativity, automation and personalization at scale

Key takeaways

  • Agents are the 2026 story. The frontier moved from chatbots to systems that plan, use tools and act inside software, coordinated through open protocols such as MCP and A2A.
  • Climb the adaptation ladder slowly. Start with prompts and context, add retrieval (RAG), then tools, and fine-tune only when the earlier steps can't close the gap.
  • No single best model. Model families now come in fast/cheap and deep-reasoning tiers, and open-weight options are competitive, so route each task to the model that fits it.
  • Evaluation is the product. Generative output can't be scored by accuracy alone. Measure groundedness, task success, latency and cost per task, and keep humans on high-risk actions.
  • Governance timelines moved. EU AI Act high-risk obligations now start in December 2027 (standalone systems) and August 2028 (embedded products). General-purpose AI model obligations have applied since August 2025.

Part 01

What's new in generative AI (November 2026)

The biggest change of the past year isn't one model. It's that AI systems now do things: navigate interfaces, call APIs, write and run code, and coordinate with other agents.

From chat to action: agents, action models and multi-agent systems

2026 generative AI trends overview slideLarge Action Models: AI that navigates interfaces and executes multi-step tasksMulti-agent systems: a manager agent coordinating specialized worker agents

Large language models reason and generate. Large Action Models and computer-use agents add the "hands": they click through applications, fill in forms, use tools and finish multi-step jobs such as booking travel inside the software that already runs the business.

Reliability comes from structure, not from one bigger prompt. In a multi-agent system, a coordinating agent breaks a goal into steps and delegates them to specialist agents (retrieval, coding, review). It then checks their output before anything leaves the system. That manager–worker pattern is now built into the major cloud agent platforms, including Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and Microsoft Foundry.

  • Open plumbing. The Model Context Protocol (MCP, how agents reach tools and data) and Agent2Agent (A2A, how agents talk to each other) both now sit under the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. MCP was donated in December 2025 and A2A joined in August 2026.
  • Tiered model families. Every major lab now ships a fast, low-cost tier next to a deep-reasoning tier, and releases arrive every few weeks rather than once a year.
  • Gated frontier capabilities. The most capable security-oriented models (OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber, Google's Gemini 3.5 Flash Cyber, Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview) are released to vetted partners first, not to everyone.

Part 02

Foundations: how generative AI works

Generative models learn the patterns in very large datasets and then produce new content (text, images, audio, video, code) that follows those patterns. Everything else in this guide builds on four ideas: what makes it generative, the architectures behind it, large language models, and foundation models.

Generative AI vs predictive AI

Typical failureMisclassificationFluent but wrong output (hallucination)
ExampleWhich customers are likely to churnA personalized retention email for each of them

The two work best together. Predictive models decide who and when, and generative models produce what.

Model architectures and the tech stack

Generative AI tech stack: infrastructure, models, tooling and applicationsGenerative AI modeling architectures: transformers, autoencoders, GANs, diffusion and reinforcement learningChoosing a generative architecture by data type and task
  • Transformers use self-attention to model long-range context in sequences. They power every major LLM. Decoder-only models (GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Qwen) excel at open-ended generation and reasoning. Encoder-decoder models (T5 family) remain strong for translation and summarization.
  • Diffusion models learn to turn noise into coherent images, video or audio, guided by a text prompt.
  • GANs and variational autoencoders were the earlier generative workhorses and are still useful for synthetic tabular and image data.
  • Mixture of Experts (MoE) activates only part of a large network for each token. Alibaba's open-weight Qwen3.5, for example, has 397B total parameters but activates 17B per token.
  • Multimodal models take in and produce several kinds of data (text, images, audio, video) in one model.
  • Reinforcement learning is how raw models are tuned to follow instructions, reason step by step and use tools.

Large language models (LLMs)

What a large language model is: trained on massive text to generate and understand languageLLM building blocks: data, architecture, training and inferenceWhat LLMs can do: generate, summarize, answer, reason and translate

A large language model is pretrained to predict the next token across a vast body of text and code. It is then refined with instruction tuning and preference training so it follows directions, reasons through problems and uses tools. Its four building blocks are data, architecture, training and inference.

In practice LLMs draft and edit content, summarize and extract from documents, answer questions over your knowledge, write and review code, generate structured data and SQL, translate, and increasingly plan and carry out multi-step tasks as agents.

Foundation models: pretrain once, adapt everywhere

Foundation models: one pretrained model adapted to many tasksFoundation model as a platform: use out of the box or build on topHow foundation models reduce labeling needs across use cases

A foundation model is a large pretrained model you adapt instead of building from scratch. Exposed to internet-scale data, it generalizes across tasks, which sharply reduces the labeled data a new use case needs. Today's examples include OpenAI's GPT family, Anthropic's Claude, Google's Gemini, Alibaba's Qwen, and Meta's Llama and Muse models.

The working pattern: plug one into your application, connect it to your knowledge (retrieval or a light fine-tune), set guardrails, and monitor quality, latency and cost as the dials you tune.

Part 03

Building with LLMs: from prompt to agent

Most teams over-engineer early. Climb the adaptation ladder one rung at a time and stop when quality is good enough.

  1. 1
    Prompt and context engineeringClear instructions, examples and the right context in the window. Fastest and cheapest.
  2. 2
    Retrieval-augmented generationGround answers in your current documents and data, with citations.
  3. 3
    Tools and function callingLet the model query systems, compute and take actions through typed interfaces.
  4. 4
    Fine-tuningTeach format, tone or narrow skills when the steps above can't get you there.
  5. 5
    Agents and orchestrationMulti-step, multi-agent workflows with evaluation and human checkpoints.

Open-weight vs closed models

How to use LLMs to build applicationsOpen-source vs closed LLMs: cost, control and effort trade-offsFactors for choosing between open and closed language models

Closed models such as GPT, Claude and Gemini are reached through an API or subscription. They offer top performance and managed safety, but limited transparency and customization. Open-weight models such as Qwen3.5 (Apache 2.0), Llama 4, Meta's Muse Glimmer and OpenAI's gpt-oss publish their weights, so you can self-host, fine-tune and keep data on your own infrastructure.

Choose on control and data residency, cost at your volume, IP and licensing risk, and your team's capacity to operate models. Hugging Face is the main hub for distributing and fine-tuning open models. It isn't a model itself.

Prompt and context engineering

Prompt engineering: designing natural-language inputs to get the results you want from an LLMPrompt engineering techniques: roles, examples and step-by-step instructionsPrompt engineering best practices for reliable LLM output

Prompt engineering means designing the instructions, examples and structure you give a model so it produces reliable, useful output without retraining. Context engineering extends this to everything in the model's window: retrieved documents, tool definitions, memory and conversation state. It is usually the fastest, lowest-cost lever, so exhaust it before reaching for retrieval pipelines or fine-tuning.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

Retrieval-augmented generation tutorial: adding knowledge to an LLMRAG overview: retrieve relevant context before generating an answerRAG architecture: ingestion, embeddings, vector index, retrieval and generation

RAG retrieves relevant passages from your own knowledge base and hands them to the model before it answers. Its key advantage over fine-tuning is freshness and control. You update knowledge by changing documents, not weights, and you decide exactly what the model may see.

Use RAG when you need domain-specific or up-to-date answers, factual accuracy, fewer hallucinations, or citations users can check. A production pipeline usually has six steps:

  1. Ingest and chunk the source content.
  2. Embed the chunks.
  3. Index them (vector, keyword or hybrid).
  4. Retrieve and re-rank for each question.
  5. Generate an answer with citations.
  6. Evaluate retrieval and answer quality continuously.

Agentic RAG, where the model decides when and what to retrieve across several steps, is now standard on the major platforms.

Vector databases and similarity search

Vector database: storing and searching high-dimensional embeddingsVector database use cases: semantic search, Q&A bots, recommendations and image searchVector database vs traditional database: similarity search vs exact match

A vector database stores embeddings: numeric representations of text, images or audio that capture meaning. It finds the closest matches with approximate nearest-neighbor search. Traditional databases excel at exact matches over rows and columns. Vector databases excel at "find things like this."

They power semantic search, question-answering bots, recommendations, visual similarity ("find designs like this one") and anomaly detection. Common choices include Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus and pgvector, and libraries such as FAISS.

Function calling and tool use

Function calling: how an LLM decides to call an external functionFunction calling flow: schema, model call, execution and responseFunction calling use cases: querying databases, fetching live data and running workflows

With function calling, you describe tools as typed schemas and the model decides when to call them and with which arguments. Your code runs the call and returns the result. This is how a model queries databases, fetches live data, runs calculations or triggers workflows, and it's the foundation of every agent. MCP now standardizes how tools are exposed, so one tool server can work across many AI applications.

Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning an OpenAI model with your own training dataWhy fine-tune: handling edge cases and complex scenariosFine-tuning workflow: dataset, training job and evaluation
Fine-tuning steps: preparing data in the required formatFine-tuning steps: creating examples for each caseFine-tuning steps: training, getting a custom model ID and testing

Fine-tuning trains a base model further on your examples so its outputs match your domain, format or voice. All major platforms support it, including OpenAI, Microsoft Foundry, Vertex AI and Bedrock, as do open-weight models through LoRA and other parameter-efficient methods. The workflow has five steps:

  1. Curate clean, representative input–output examples in the required JSONL format.
  2. Upload and validate them.
  3. Run the training job.
  4. Swap in the resulting custom model ID.
  5. Evaluate against real prompts and iterate.

Try prompting, examples and RAG first. They're faster, cheaper and easier to change, and they tell you whether fine-tuning is needed at all.

AI assistant trade-offs: accuracy, performance and cost

AI assistant trade-off factors: accuracy, performance and costAI assistant trade-offs when optimizing for performance and speedAI assistant trade-offs when optimizing for accuracy

Every assistant balances three dials, and you rarely get all three at their best:

  • Accuracy builds trust and lets the assistant handle complex tasks.
  • Performance (speed and scale) shapes user experience and how many requests you can serve.
  • Cost drives adoption and ROI.

Decide which dial matters most for each use case. Then use model tiers and routing to buy accuracy only where it pays.

Part 06

Evaluate, secure and govern

Generative systems can be confidently wrong, can be manipulated through their inputs, and now take actions. Evaluation, guardrails, governance and security are what make them safe to deploy.

How to evaluate generative AI

How to evaluate generative AI: decide what matters firstGenerative AI evaluation criteria: creativity, realism and diversityWhy generative models are hard to benchmark compared with traditional ML

Traditional ML is scored against a known right answer. Generative AI often has many acceptable answers, so start by deciding what "good" means for the task, then measure it. A practical evaluation stack has four layers:

  • Golden sets. Curated questions with reference answers or rubrics, rerun on every model or prompt change.
  • Task metrics. Groundedness and faithfulness to sources, task success rate, latency, and cost per completed task.
  • Model-graded and human review. An LLM judge calibrated against human ratings, with humans owning the edge cases.
  • Online signals. A/B tests, user feedback and production traces.

For creative generation, add realism, diversity and novelty.

Guardrails for LLMs and chatbots

Guardrails for LLMs: steering outputs in the desired directionChallenges in implementing LLM guardrailsSolutions and techniques for LLM guardrails

Guardrails are the controls that keep a model on track. They validate inputs, filter outputs, monitor usage in real time and keep humans involved where the stakes are high. Agents raise the bar, because a bad output can now be a bad action.

Risks, trade-offs and open questions

Generative AI trade-offs and conflicts to consider before enterprise useGenerative AI challenges: ethics, copyright, data ownership and environmentGenerative AI challenges overview slide
Uncontrolled data generation: limited control over what generative models produceEthical issues in generative AI: bias inherited from training dataData ownership and copyright questions for AI-generated content
Environmental concerns: compute, energy use and carbon emissions of generative AILegal questions around generative AI useUsing generative AI responsibly
  • Unpredictable output. Models produce fluent variation with limited control. That's a problem in mission-critical settings until grounding, evaluation and human review are in place.
  • Bias. Models inherit the biases of their training data. They need testing and mitigation, not assumptions.
  • Copyright and data ownership. Who owns generated content, and what training data may be used, is still being settled in courts and legislatures.
  • Security. Prompt injection, data leakage and over-privileged agents are the new attack surface.
  • Environmental cost. Training and serving large models takes significant compute and energy. Use the smallest model that does the job, and generate with purpose rather than for endless variation.

Governance frameworks for GenAI, LLMs and chatbots

Good governance turns principles (fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, safety) into specific controls for each kind of use: personalization, automation and creative generation. The deep-dive slides map each scenario to its governance items.

Regulatory clock: EU AI Act

  • Since 2 August 2025: obligations for general-purpose AI models apply.
  • 2 December 2027: standalone high-risk systems (Annex III, e.g. hiring, credit scoring, education) must comply. This was deferred from August 2026 by the Digital Omnibus on AI, which received final approval in June 2026.
  • 2 August 2028: high-risk AI embedded in regulated products (Annex I) must comply.

The delay is runway, not a reprieve. Inventory, risk classification and documentation take longer than most teams expect.

Security framework for generative AI

FAQ

Generative AI 101: frequently asked questions

What is generative AI?

Generative AI is a class of AI models that learn patterns from large datasets and produce new content (text, code, images, audio or video) in response to a prompt. Large language models are the best-known example. Today's systems also use tools and act inside software as agents.

How is generative AI different from predictive AI?

Predictive AI analyzes historical data to output a label, score or forecast, such as which customers are likely to churn. Generative AI creates new content, such as a personalized retention email. Predictive models are scored against ground truth. Generative models are evaluated on groundedness, task success and quality.

What is the difference between RAG and fine-tuning?

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fetches relevant documents at query time and gives them to the model, so knowledge stays current and auditable. Fine-tuning changes the model's weights with training examples to teach format, tone or narrow skills. Try prompting and RAG first, and fine-tune only when they can't close the gap.

Which LLM should I use in 2026?

There is no single best model. Leading families include OpenAI GPT-6, Anthropic Claude (Haiku, Sonnet and Opus 5.5, and Fable 5.2), Google Gemini 3.x and open-weight models such as Qwen3.5. Evaluate candidates on your own tasks, then route fast, cheap tasks to small tiers and hard reasoning to frontier tiers.

What are MCP and A2A?

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes how AI applications connect to tools and data. Agent2Agent (A2A) standardizes how independent agents communicate. Both are now hosted by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, which makes multi-vendor agent systems easier to build.

What are the biggest risks of generative AI in the enterprise?

Hallucinated or uncontrolled output, bias inherited from training data, data leakage and privacy issues, prompt injection against agents, unsettled copyright and data-ownership questions, and environmental cost. Guardrails, grounding, evaluation, human review of high-risk actions and a governance framework address them.

When do the EU AI Act's high-risk rules apply?

Under the Digital Omnibus on AI, approved in June 2026, obligations for standalone high-risk AI systems (Annex III) apply from 2 December 2027, and those for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products (Annex I) from 2 August 2028. Obligations for general-purpose AI models have applied since 2 August 2025.
