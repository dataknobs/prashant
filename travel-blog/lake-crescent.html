A lake clear enough to see nearly twenty metres down.

Lake Crescent sits entirely within Olympic National Park, about 17-18 miles west of Port Angeles along US-101, which traces roughly ten miles of the lake's southern shore in a series of scenic curves. What sets it apart from most lakes is simple but striking: the water is exceptionally clear, with visibility extending down as far as 60 feet (about 18 metres) — clear enough that on a calm day, the lake bed and submerged rock formations remain visible far below the surface.

The explanation is partly chemical and partly a matter of depth. The lake's water contains unusually low levels of nitrogen, which limits the growth of algae that would otherwise cloud the water. Combined with the lake's considerable depth — an official maximum of 624 feet (190m), making it the second deepest lake in Washington after Lake Chelan — the cold, still water absorbs most wavelengths of light except blue, which reflects back to produce the lake's deep sapphire colour. Some unofficial soundings during cable-laying work suggested depths beyond 1,000 feet, though these readings were never verified and the true maximum depth remains uncertain.

The basin itself was carved by glaciers during the last Ice Age, and Lake Crescent was originally part of a single larger lake shared with neighbouring Lake Sutherland to the east — until an ancient landslide split the two apart. Local Klallam tribal legend attributes the split to Mount Storm King, said to have divided the warring Quileute and Klallam tribes by separating the lake between them. That isolation had an unexpected consequence: two trout subspecies, the Beardslee trout and Crescenti trout, evolved independently within Lake Crescent and exist nowhere else on Earth.

Around the lake, dense old-growth forest — western red cedar, Douglas fir, and other Pacific Northwest conifers — comes right down to the shoreline in most places, with the Marymere Falls trail (1.8 miles round trip from the Storm King Ranger Station) winding through some of the most impressive stands to a 90-foot waterfall tucked into a side canyon.

Visitor tip: Swimming is allowed and popular in summer, though surface water stays cold (typically low-to-mid 50s°F) even at the height of the season due to the lake's depth. The historic Lake Crescent Lodge, dating to 1916, rents rowboats and kayaks seasonally — the sheltered eastern end near the lodge and Barnes Point offers the calmest paddling conditions.

Why is Lake Crescent's water so clear and blue?

The clarity comes from unusually low nitrogen levels, which limit algae growth. Combined with the lake's depth, the clear, cold water absorbs most light wavelengths except blue, which reflects back, giving the lake its sapphire colour. Visibility extends down as far as 60 feet (~18m).

How deep is Lake Crescent?

The official maximum depth is 624 feet (190m) — the second deepest lake in Washington after Lake Chelan. A 2013-14 survey measured 596 feet. Earlier unofficial soundings suggested over 1,000 feet, but these were never verified. Average depth is around 300 feet (91m).

How was Lake Crescent formed?

The lake occupies a basin carved by glaciers during the last Ice Age. It was originally one larger lake with neighbouring Lake Sutherland to the east, until an ancient landslide split the two — a split local Klallam legend attributes to Mount Storm King.

What fish live in Lake Crescent?

Lake Crescent is home to the Beardslee trout and Crescenti trout — two subspecies found nowhere else on Earth, which evolved in isolation after the lake's separation from Lake Sutherland.

Can you swim, and how do you get there?

Yes — swimming is popular in summer, though the surface stays cold (low-to-mid 50s°F) due to the lake's depth. Devil's Punch Bowl is a popular swimming/diving spot. The lake is on US-101, about 17-18 miles west of Port Angeles, accessible without entering the park's fee area (some trailheads require a pass).
