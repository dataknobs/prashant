By Prashant Dhingra · DataKnobs · Updated November 17, 2026 · Part of Generative AI 101

Traditional machine learning has a ground truth to score against. Generative AI often has many acceptable answers, and agents add actions on top. That makes evaluation the real product work: deciding what "good" means, measuring it continuously, and building guardrails that stop bad outputs and bad actions before they reach people or systems.

Key takeaways

  • Define quality per task before choosing metrics. Groundedness, task success, safety, latency and cost are the usual core.
  • A golden set is your most valuable asset. Rerun it on every prompt, model or retrieval change.
  • LLM judges scale review, but only after you calibrate them against human ratings.
  • Layer guardrails: input checks, grounding, output filters and action gates, each catching what the others miss.

Why evaluating generative AI is different

How to evaluate generative AI: decide what matters firstGenerative AI evaluation criteria: creativity, realism and diversityWhy generative models are hard to benchmark compared with traditional ML

A spam classifier is right or wrong. A summary, an answer or an agent's plan can be good in many ways and bad in many more. Evaluation therefore starts with a decision: what matters for this task? Factual grounding, completeness, tone, format, safety, speed and cost all compete. For creative generation, realism, diversity and novelty matter too, and those are harder still to benchmark.

Building a golden set

  1. Collect real inputsPull 50–300 representative requests from logs, pilots or subject-matter experts.
  2. Cover the edgesAdd hard cases, ambiguous requests, out-of-scope questions and known past failures.
  3. Write references or rubricsGive ideal answers where they exist; otherwise write scoring criteria experts agree on.
  4. Version itStore it with your code, and add every production failure you fix.
  5. Gate releases on itNo prompt, model or retrieval change ships without a comparison run.

Using an LLM as a judge

Human review doesn't scale to thousands of runs, so teams use a strong model to grade outputs against a rubric. It works well if you treat the judge itself as something to evaluate:

  • Give the judge a specific rubric and the source material, and ask for a short justification with the score.
  • Compare judge scores with human ratings on a sample, and only trust the judge where they agree.
  • Watch for known biases: preference for longer answers, for its own model family, and for the first option shown.
  • Use pairwise comparisons (A vs B) when you're choosing between versions.

Evaluating agents and running online tests

For agents, score the outcome (was the task done correctly?) and the trajectory (were the steps sensible, efficient and within policy?). Run agents in a sandbox that mirrors production, and check the end state of the systems they touched.

Offline tests catch regressions; online tests show real value. Use shadow deployments, A/B tests and user feedback, and feed production traces back into the golden set. Managed agent platforms now build this in: Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, for example, made batch evaluations, recommendations and A/B tests generally available in June 2026.

A layered guardrail architecture

Guardrails are the controls that steer outputs and actions in the right direction. No single filter is enough, so stack them:

  1. 1
    InputValidate, scope and sanitize requests; detect jailbreaks and injected instructions.
  2. 2
    GroundingRetrieve trusted sources; require citations; allow "not found".
  3. 3
    ModelA well-chosen model, a tested system prompt and safe defaults.
  4. 4
    OutputClassifiers for safety, PII and policy; schema and fact checks.
  5. 5
    ActionLeast privilege, approval gates, budgets and full audit logs.

Red-teaming and monitoring

  • Attack your own system before launch: jailbreaks, prompt injection through documents and web pages, data-exfiltration attempts, harmful requests and tool abuse.
  • Turn every successful attack into a test case in your golden or safety set.
  • Monitor in production: quality scores, safety flags, refusal and escalation rates, latency and cost, with alerts on regressions.
  • Review samples by hand every week, even when the dashboards look green.

Evaluation and guardrails: frequently asked questions

How do you evaluate an LLM application?

Define what good means for the task, build a golden set of realistic inputs with reference answers or rubrics, and score each version for groundedness, correctness, format, safety, latency and cost. Use calibrated automated grading plus human review, and gate every release on the results.

What is LLM-as-a-judge?

LLM-as-a-judge uses a capable language model to score outputs against a rubric. It scales evaluation but must be calibrated against human ratings and checked for biases such as favoring longer answers.

What are AI guardrails?

AI guardrails are the controls around a model that keep it within policy: input validation, grounding to trusted sources, output filters, privacy protections, and limits and approvals on actions. They work best in layers.

How do you evaluate an AI agent?

Score both the outcome, whether the task was completed correctly, and the trajectory, whether the steps and tool calls were sensible and within policy, in a sandbox that mirrors production. Track success rate, steps per task, tool error rate and cost per task.
